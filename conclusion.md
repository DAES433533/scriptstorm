Across all three meteorological events the July 2023 Western U.S. heatwave, the 2011 tornado outbreak, and the November 2022 Buffalo lake effect snowstorm, our analysis using ERA5 reanalysis data, satellite observations, and radar imagery highlights how different atmospheric setups lead to dramatically different impacts.

For the July 2023 heatwave, ERA5 cloud cover fields showed widespread clear skies across the western United States, a signal that was confirmed using GOES 18 true color imagery. The combination of clear skies, strong solar heating, and elevated 850 mb temperatures aligned with the presence of a pronounced 500 hPa ridge, which explains the intense and persistent heat experienced across the region. Wind vectors at 850 mb also supported the stagnant, subsident air mass typical of strong ridging and heatwaves.

During the April 2011 tornado outbreak, ERA5 fields captured the classic severe weather environment, with high surface temperatures across the Southeast and central U.S., and CAPE values ranging from 500 to 2000 J/kg, indicating sufficient instability for tornadic supercells. The 850 mb level showed strong southerly flow transporting warm, moist Gulf air northward, fueling storm development, while sea level pressure fields revealed a deep low with central pressures near 980 hPa. Radar imagery over Alabama, particularly near Tuscaloosa, confirmed the severity of the event, showing well defined hook echoes and strong rotational signatures associated with the EF4 tornado that caused catastrophic damage. Observations of the supercells during this outbreak emphasize how the combination of deep layer shear and high instability, and storm relative helicity contributes to the development of violent tornadoes (Lyza  2022). These findings align closely with the meteorological overview provided by (Knupp 2014), who highlighted the extreme synoptic and mesoscale environment on 27 April 2011 including a negatively tilted upper level trough, a strong 850 mb low level jet, CAPE values of 2500 to 3000 J/kg, low lifting condensation levels, and intense low level wind shear all factors that supported the formation of long track, violent tornadoes.

The November 2022 Buffalo lake effect snowstorm displayed an entirely different but equally impressive set of atmospheric dynamics. ERA5 snow depth analyses indicated over 30 inches of accumulation just south of Buffalo, while radar data showed the persistent and nearly stationary lake effect band that lasted nearly two days. According to (CBS News 2022), some areas south of Buffalo received more than 5 feet of snow, with Orchard Park reporting 77 inches and the town of Natural Bridge just under 6 feet. The storm caused at least three fatalities and forced major disruptions, including the relocation of an NFL game, highlighting the severe societal impacts of lake effect snow. Geopotential heights dropped from around the 540 dam line to near 530 dam between November 18 to 19, signaling a colder air mass settling into the region. The 850 mb temperatures and westerly flow supported ideal lake effect conditions with cold air aloft passing over the warm lake surface generating intense convection and extremely localized snowfall. The CBS report also emphasizes how highly localized snow bands characteristic of lake effect events can produce extreme variability in snowfall over short distances aligning with our 

ERA5 and radar observations.
The three events highlight the wide range of atmospheric conditions that produce significant weather impacts across the United States. From extreme heat to violent tornadoes to localized lake effect snow, ERA5, satellite imagery, and radar data collectively reveal the underlying dynamics that shaped each outcome. 

Working with ERA5, GOES imagery, and radar taught us how to build a consistent workflow from pulling reanalysis data to plotting synoptic fields generating satellite loops and interpreting radar structures that can be applied to a wide range of atmospheric case studies. For future teams or readers this setup gives a solid starting point to dig into more advanced weather cases. The same steps we used here can be applied to other major events like atmospheric rivers, derechos, big blizzards, or even hurricanes.
